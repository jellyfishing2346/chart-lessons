Good charts vs bad charts

Every chart tells a story. A good chart tells a true story you can see at a glance. A misleading chart tells a story the numbers don’t actually support. Each example below is an everyday situation drawn two ways, using exactly the same numbers.

Choose your level

Three kinds of charts you’ll see

Bar chart
Bars of different lengths. Longer bar = bigger number. Good for comparing amounts.
Line chart
A line that goes up and down over time. Good for showing change.
Scatter plot
Lots of dots. Each dot is one thing, like one player. Good for seeing if two things go together.

How to read any chart

Use these every time you see a chart, in class, in the news or on social media.

Think like a reporter

  1. What do you notice? What jumps out first? What’s the biggest, smallest, highest or lowest?
  2. What do you wonder? What questions does it raise? What’s missing?
  3. What’s the headline? Sum up the chart’s story in one catchy sentence, like a news headline.

Then check: is the story true?

  1. Do the bars start at zero? If not, the bars can trick your eyes.
  2. What do the colors mean? Different colors should mean different groups.
  3. Is it easy to read? If you can’t find the answer fast, the chart isn’t doing its job.
  1. Where does the axis start? Bars should start at zero, or their lengths lie.
  2. Is there more than one axis? Two axes can be stretched to make any two lines look connected.
  3. What do the colors mean? A new color should mean a new group, nothing else.
  4. Is it a real change or just noise? Bumpy data needs a trend line, not a zig-zag.
  5. Is it easy to read? If it takes more than a few seconds to find the answer, the chart is getting in the way.
🌳 Go deeper: questions experts ask
  • Who made this chart, and what do they want you to believe? A campaign, a company and a scientist might draw the same data very differently.
  • How much data is behind it? A survey of a few people can swing a lot by chance.
  • Is it a total or a rate? A big school will have more of almost everything; “per 100 students” is often fairer.
  • What’s left out? A chart that shows only a few months can hide the bigger picture.
  • Does it show cause, or just things happening together? Two lines moving together don’t prove one caused the other.

Student council election

Did Maya win in a landslide?

Maya got — votes, Jordan got — and Sam got —.
🔍 Spot it: In the misleading chart, how many times taller is Maya’s bar than Sam’s? Does that match the votes?

✗ Misleading

This chart says: Maya won in a landslide!

Why is this misleading?

The axis starts at 40 instead of zero. Cutting off the bottom of the bars makes Maya’s bar look many times taller than Sam’s, even though the vote counts are close.

✓ Honest

This chart says: Maya won a close race.

Why it works: the bars start at zero, so a bar twice as tall really means twice the votes. The winner is highlighted and the vote counts are written on top.

🌳 Go deeperWhy does cutting the axis fool us? We judge bars by their length, so chopping off the bottom changes how they compare. Line charts are different: we read their slope and position, so a line chart of something like body temperature doesn’t need to start at zero. The advanced quiz tests this.

Rule: bar charts always start at zero.

Screen time and test scores

Is screen time wrecking test scores?

Since —, students’ daily screen time went up —, and average test scores went down —.
🔍 Spot it: The lines make a dramatic X. Did both things really change by about the same amount?

✗ Misleading

This chart says: Screen time is wrecking test scores!

Why is this misleading?

Each line has its own axis, and each axis was stretched to fill the chart. That makes a small dip in scores look as big as a huge jump in screen time, so it looks like one is causing the other.

✓ Honest

This chart says: Screen time rose a lot. Scores dipped a little.

Why it works: both lines are measured the same way, as percent change since the first year, on one shared axis. Now you can compare them fairly. (Even an honest chart like this can’t prove one thing caused the other.)

🌳 Go deeperEven the honest chart only shows that screen time and scores moved at the same time. That’s correlation, not proof of cause. What else could explain both? Maybe a few years of remote school, or more homework done online. A good chart-maker says what the chart can and can’t prove.

Rule: be suspicious when two lines match perfectly. Check the axes.

Weekly cafeteria survey

Is school lunch getting better or worse?

Every week, a few students are asked if lunch was good. The chart shows the percent who said yes.
🔍 Spot it: Did lunch really get much better, then much worse, almost every week?

✗ Misleading

This chart says: Lunch is a roller coaster!

Why is this misleading?

Each week only a few students are asked, so the result bounces around by chance. Connecting every point turns that random bounce into fake drama, and the narrow axis makes the swings look even bigger.

✓ Honest

This chart says: Lunch is slowly getting better.

Why it works: each week is a faint dot, and the solid line is the average of nearby weeks. The average smooths out the random bounce and shows the real trend.

🌳 Go deeperWhy does the survey bounce? With only a handful of students asked each week, results naturally swing by several points. That’s called sampling error, and polls report it as a margin of error. The moving average trades noise for delay: it reacts to real changes a little late.

Rule: show the trend, not the noise.

Basketball team

Does more practice mean more points?

Each dot is a player: hours of practice per week, and points scored per game. The coach wants to talk about four players.
🔍 Spot it: In the misleading chart, what do you think the orange players have in common?

✗ Misleading

This chart says: The orange players are a special group.

Why is this misleading?

Orange looks like it means something, like starters or a different team. But it only marks the players who have name labels. A color that means nothing sends readers looking for a story that isn’t there.

✓ Honest

This chart says: More practice usually means more points, but Isla stands out.

Why it works: every player is the same color. Players we aren’t talking about fade into the background, and Isla, the surprise in the story, is in bold: she scores far more than her practice time would predict.

🌳 Go deeperIsla is an outlier, a point far from the pattern. Outliers are often the most interesting part of a story, but before you build a headline on one, ask: is it a mistake in the data, a fluke, or something real? What would you want to know about Isla?

Rule: a new color should mean a new group.

Class survey

What’s our favorite after-school activity?

Every student picked one favorite activity. The chart shows the percent who picked each.
🔍 Spot it: Which activity came third? How long did it take you to find it?

✗ Hard to read

This chart says: Um… lots of different things?

Why is this hard to read?

Our eyes are bad at comparing the sizes of slices, and you have to keep looking back and forth at the color key. Music and Art look almost the same. The chart hides its own story.

✓ Easy to read

This chart says: Sports and gaming are the class favorites.

Why it works: the bars are sorted from most to least popular and labeled directly, so the ranking reads top to bottom in one second.

🌳 Go deeperResearchers William Cleveland and Robert McGill (1984) tested how accurately people read different kinds of charts. People judged position and length (bars, dots) much more accurately than angles and areas (pie slices, bubbles). That’s the science behind the rule.

Rule: to compare more than a few things, use sorted bars.

Tell your own story with a chart

Making a chart? Work through these steps in order. Most bad charts happen when people skip step 2.

  1. Ask a question. “What’s our class’s favorite activity?”
  2. Write your answer as a headline. “Sports is our favorite activity.”
  3. Draw a chart that shows it. Use bars that start at zero, and put your headline on top.
  1. Start with a question. “Did Maya win easily?” “Is lunch getting better?”
  2. Write your headline first. Answer your question in one sentence. That’s the story your chart has to prove.
  3. Pick the chart that fits the story. Comparing amounts → bar chart (starting at zero). Change over time → line chart. Do two things go together? → scatter plot. Parts of a whole → sorted bars, or a pie with only two or three slices.
  4. Point at the story. Use your headline as the chart’s title. Color the one thing that matters and make everything else gray. Label things directly instead of using a color key.
  5. Check it’s honest. Run through the five checks at the top of this page. Would the story still hold if someone saw the full numbers?

Strong chart-makers also show uncertainty (“about 1 in 4, give or take”), think about their audience (a principal and a 6th grader need different charts), and name their sources so readers can check the data.

Word list

Chart words used on this page, in plain English. More words appear at higher levels.

Axis
The scale along the side or bottom of a chart that tells you what the numbers are.
Baseline
Where the axis starts, usually zero. Bars grow up (or across) from it.
Truncated axis
An axis that has been cut off so it doesn’t start at zero.
Dual axis
A chart with two different y-axes, one on each side.
Percent change
How much something went up or down compared with where it started. +50% means it grew by half.
Trend line
A smooth line that shows the general direction of bumpy data.
Moving average
The average of a few nearby values, worked out again at every point. It smooths out the bounce.
Noise
Random ups and downs that don’t mean anything real.
Outlier
A value much bigger or smaller than the rest. Often the most interesting part of the story.
Legend
The key that tells you what each color or symbol means.
Data label
A number written right on the chart, next to the bar or point.
Headline
One sentence that sums up the story a chart is telling.
Correlation
When two things tend to go up or down together. It doesn’t prove one causes the other.
Sample size
How many people or things were measured. Small samples bounce around more.
Margin of error
How far a survey result could be off just by chance, like “45%, give or take 3.”
Rate vs total
A total counts everything; a rate divides by size (“per 100 students”) so big and small groups can be compared fairly.
Cherry-picking
Showing only the part of the data that supports your story, like a few bad months out of three good years.
