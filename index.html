Good charts vs bad charts

5 questions to ask about any chart

Use these as a checklist whenever you read or make a chart.

  1. Where does the axis start? Bars should start at zero, or their lengths lie.
  2. Is there more than one axis? Two axes can be stretched to make any two lines look connected.
  3. What do the colours mean? A new colour should mean a new group, nothing else.
  4. Is it a real change or just noise? Bumpy data needs a trend line, not a zig-zag.
  5. What is the one point? If you can't say it in one sentence, the chart is too busy.

Bar charts that don't start at zero

The point is how far the leader's posts outperform everyone else's.
🔍 Spot it: In the left chart, how many times bigger does the leader's bar look than MP A's?

✗ Bad
What's wrong?

The axis starts well above zero. The leader's bar runs off the edge, and the small bars look much bigger than they really are.

✓ Better

Bars start at zero, so their lengths are honest. Numbers are written on the bars, so no axis is needed.

Rule: bar charts always start at zero.

Two y-axes stretched to match

Two measures of dog size over time.
🔍 Spot it: The two lines look identical. Did weight and neck size really shrink by the same amount?

✗ Bad
What's wrong?

Each side has its own axis, stretched until the lines overlap. It looks like a perfect match, but weight fell —, neck size only —, over the same years.

✓ Better

Both lines start at 100 (the first year) on one shared axis, so you can see which one really fell more.

Rule: be suspicious when two lines match perfectly. Check the axes.

Joining every poll with a line

Weekly polls asking whether a decision was right or wrong.
🔍 Spot it: Did people really change their minds every single week?

✗ Bad
What's wrong?

Polls go up and down a little just by chance. Joining every dot makes that random wobble look like real mood swings.

✓ Better

Each poll is a faint dot. The solid line is the average of nearby polls, which shows the real trend.

Rule: show the trend, not the noise.

Colour that suggests a group that isn't there

Pension spending vs share of population aged 65+, with one focus country.
🔍 Spot it: What do you think the bright dots have in common?

✗ Bad
What's wrong?

The bright dots look like a special group, but they're only the ones with name labels. The colour suggests something that isn't true.

✓ Better

All dots are the same colour. Less important ones are lighter, and the country we care about is in bold.

Rule: a new colour should mean a new group.

Pie chart with too many slices

Market share across nine products.
🔍 Spot it: Which product is the third largest? How long did it take you?

✗ Bad
What's wrong?

You have to compare angles and keep looking back at the colour key. Our eyes are bad at both.

✓ Better

Bars sorted from biggest to smallest, with labels on them. The answer takes one second.

Rule: if you need to compare more than a few parts, use bars.

Word list

Chart words used on this page, in plain English.

Axis
The scale along the side or bottom of a chart that tells you what the numbers are.
Baseline
Where the axis starts, usually zero. Bars grow up (or across) from it.
Truncated axis
An axis that has been cut so it doesn't start at zero.
Dual axis
A chart with two different y-axes, one on each side.
Trend line
A smooth line that shows the general direction of bumpy data.
Moving average
The average of a few nearby values, worked out again at every point. It smooths out the wobble.
Index (first year = 100)
Turning numbers into "compared with the start": 90 means 10% lower than the first year.
Outlier
A value much bigger or smaller than the rest.
Legend
The key that tells you what each colour or symbol means.
Data label
A number written right on the chart, next to the bar or point.
Noise
Random ups and downs that don't mean anything real.

Practice charts use made-up numbers. The mistake list draws on The Economist's "Mistakes, we've drawn a few" (Sarah Leo, 2019) and the sources under "Where to learn more".
